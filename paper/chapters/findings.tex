% ===========================================================================
\section{Key Findings}
\label{sec:findings}
% ===========================================================================

Our evaluation reveals several findings with implications for distributed
system design, deployment, and monitoring.

\subsection{Finding 1: Sub-20\,ms Danger Zones Are Universal Across Consensus}

Five of ten systems (etcd, ZooKeeper, MongoDB, TiKV, CockroachDB), all
Raft-based or equivalent consensus protocols, trigger catastrophic leader
elections at $\leq$19.5\,ms of network delay. The true boundary is likely
even lower (see Section~\ref{sec:head-to-head}, boundary interpretation).

This is \textbf{250$\times$ below} typical production alerting thresholds.
Industry monitoring platforms document default alert configurations of
1--5\,s for network latency anomalies~\cite{datadog-monitoring}: Datadog's
default network monitor triggers at 3\,s~\cite{datadog-monitoring}, PagerDuty
recommends 5\,s thresholds for latency alerts~\cite{pagerduty-best-practices},
and the Google SRE book recommends alerting on ``significant'' latency
increases typically defined as $>$1\,s~\cite{google-sre-book}.

\para{Root cause} Consensus protocols use heartbeat intervals (typically
100--500\,ms) to detect peer failure. When added delay causes heartbeat
round-trip time to exceed the election timeout, followers initiate elections.
The critical insight is that the \emph{added} delay compounds with existing
network latency and processing time: even a few milliseconds of additional
delay can tip a marginally-timed heartbeat over the election threshold during
load spikes.

\para{System-specific mechanisms}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{etcd 3.5.10}: Heartbeat interval 100\,ms, election timeout
        1000\,ms (10$\times$ heartbeat). Under YCSB load, processing time
        already consumes most of the 100\,ms budget; 19.5\,ms additional delay
        causes missed heartbeats, accumulating to election timeout.
  \item \textbf{ZooKeeper 3.8}: \texttt{tickTime} 2000\,ms with
        \texttt{syncLimit}=5 ticks. Session timeouts interact with election
        protocol; delay causes followers to enter LOOKING state.
  \item \textbf{MongoDB 7.0}: Default \texttt{electionTimeoutMillis} 10\,s,
        but heartbeat frequency is 2\,s. Slow heartbeat responses trigger
        ``not primary'' cascades before the full timeout expires.
  \item \textbf{TiKV 7.5}: PD (Placement Driver) uses store heartbeats to
        detect slow stores. The ``slow score'' mechanism is more sensitive
        than the raw election timeout.
  \item \textbf{CockroachDB 23.2}: Multi-Raft with per-range leaders. Network
        delay affects multiple ranges simultaneously, amplifying the failure
        surface.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Finding 2: Timeout Architecture Determines Resilience}

Redis Cluster's danger zone (2.7\,s) is \textbf{138$\times$ higher} than
consensus-based systems. This dramatic difference traces to a fundamental
architectural choice:

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Failure detection architectures and their danger-zone implications.}
\label{tab:architectures}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Architecture} & \textbf{Boundary} & \textbf{Systems} \\
\midrule
Heartbeat-sensitive & $\leq$19.5\,ms & etcd, ZK, MongoDB, \\
consensus & & TiKV, CockroachDB \\
\midrule
$\phi$-accrual & 39\,ms & Cassandra \\
detector & & \\
\midrule
ZK-coordinated & 39--313\,ms & HBase, Hadoop, \\
session timeout & & Kafka \\
\midrule
Explicit timeout & 2.7\,s & Redis Cluster \\
with gossip & & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\para{Heartbeat-sensitive consensus} (Raft, ZAB, Multi-Raft) ties liveness
detection directly to the consensus protocol's heartbeat mechanism. This
provides fast failover ($<$10\,s) but creates extreme sensitivity to
latency perturbation.

\para{$\phi$-accrual failure detector} (Cassandra) uses statistical modeling
of inter-arrival times to adaptively threshold failure detection. This provides
moderate jitter tolerance (39\,ms boundary) while maintaining reasonable
detection speed.

\para{ZK-coordinated sessions} (HBase, Hadoop, Kafka pre-KRaft) delegate
failure detection to ZooKeeper session timeouts (typically 30\,s). Individual
components tolerate significant delay as long as their ZK session remains
alive, but session expiry is catastrophic.

\para{Explicit timeout with gossip} (Redis) uses configurable
\texttt{cluster-node-timeout} (default 5\,s) requiring \emph{sustained}
unreachability exceeding timeout/2 before PFAIL marking. This deliberate
design trades detection speed for jitter tolerance.

\para{Design implication} System designers face an inherent trade-off:
\begin{equation}
\text{Detection Latency} \propto \text{Jitter Tolerance}
\end{equation}

\system{}'s minimal recipes quantify this trade-off precisely for each system,
enabling informed decisions about timeout configuration.

\subsection{Finding 3: Filesystem Faults Are 4--16$\times$ More Tolerable}

Across all systems, filesystem fault boundaries are 4--16$\times$ higher
than network fault boundaries:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
System & NW & FS & Ratio \\
\midrule
etcd & 19.5\,ms & 78.1\,ms & 4$\times$ \\
ZooKeeper & 19.5\,ms & 156.3\,ms & 8$\times$ \\
MongoDB & 19.5\,ms & 312.5\,ms & 16$\times$ \\
HBase & 39.1\,ms & 78.1\,ms & 2$\times$ \\
Kafka & 78.1\,ms & 625.0\,ms & 8$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\para{Explanation} Network heartbeats are latency-critical (sub-second
timeouts), while I/O operations are expected to have variable latency.
Systems configure generous I/O timeouts (seconds to minutes) because disk
operations are inherently bursty. This creates a larger safety margin for
filesystem degradation before it triggers system-level failures.

\para{Exception: HBase} Shows only 2$\times$ ratio because its WAL
(Write-Ahead Log) writes are in the critical path of RPC handling. Slow WAL
fsync directly delays RPC responses, which cascades to ZooKeeper session
maintenance.

\subsection{Finding 4: Multi-Fault Boundaries Are Significantly Lower}

Combined faults lower individual boundaries by mean 31.4\%
(Section~\ref{sec:multi-fault-eval}). The most severe case (HBase nw+fs:
50\% reduction) demonstrates that production environments (which routinely
experience multiple simultaneous degradations) are more vulnerable than
single-fault testing suggests.

\para{Implication for CI} Regression tests based on single-fault boundaries
may provide false confidence. We recommend testing at 70\% of the single-fault
boundary to account for multi-fault interaction effects:
\begin{equation}
\text{CI threshold} = 0.7 \times \text{single-fault boundary}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Finding 5: The Minimizer Is CI-Practical}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
  \item Average: 17.8 iterations per system for network faults.
  \item Wall time: 7--14 minutes per system (including full cluster lifecycle).
  \item Total: 99 minutes for all 10 systems (one fault type).
  \item All five fault types across 10 systems: $\sim$8 hours.
\end{itemize}

A nightly CI job running \system{} against all production systems produces
up-to-date minimal fault specifications in under one shift. Weekly runs track
boundary drift across versions, configuration changes, and infrastructure
updates.
